Energy beyond Earth
HAPS Power Beaming · Capability Study

What capabilities could power beaming provide to HAPS?

Today, HAPS cannot maintain year-round flight in mid-to-high latitudes, batteries take up to a third of the airframe, and fragile lightweight builds mean launches must wait for calm air. Power beaming can lift all three limits.

Capability 1
Year-round flight at any latitude. Close the winter deficit that grounds high-latitude HAPS for months.
Capability 2
Less battery, more payload. Redirect energy storage mass into sensors.
Capability 3
Sturdier airframe. Beamed power eases engineering constraints, enabling take-off in real weather.

Section 1 · The solar trap

Latitude and season limit flight.

A solar HAPS flies only when the daily energy budget allows. At 50°N, winter Sun is insufficient; at 60°N this can last months.

The valuable missions – polar ISR, Arctic science, maritime surveillance – sit exactly where solar power fails.

Section 2 · The mass spiral

Battery weight is a large portion of airframe.

A light solar plane carries ~25 kg of battery on a 75 kg maximum takeoff weight – a third of the airframe, just to make it through the night.

Beamed power works day or night, easing the energy storage requirements.

Section 3 · Ground-up beaming

A laser from the ground, a receiver under the wing.

A ground station – fixed, shipborne, or on a mobile mast – points a laser at a PV receiver mounted on the underside of the airframe. Slant range is just 20–80 km, so the beam stays tight with a modest transmitter aperture.

Cleanest atmospheric path possible, but bound to line-of-sight and clear sky at the ground.

Ground-up architecture

A single ground station serves any HAPS within its line-of-sight window.

Section 4 · Space-down beaming

A LEO transmitter constellation serving a HAPS constellation.

A constellation of LEO transmitters serves a constellation of HAPS. Each satellite passes overhead briefly but frequently; coordinated across the constellation, every HAPS sees a transmitter many times per day.

Global coverage in one system, including the high-latitude regions ground stations cannot easily reach. The path is mostly above weather.

Space-down architecture

Each HAPS sees several transmitter passes per day; the constellation keeps power flowing globally.

Section 5 · What it unlocks

Three constraints lifted at once.

Persistence. A four-month polar platform becomes a 365-day asset.

Payload. Freed battery mass becomes payload mass – 1:1, no propulsion changes.

Robustness. Spend freed up mass on structure instead, and the launch envelope widens markedly: a heavier, sturdier airframe tolerates higher gust speeds, so launches and recoveries are no longer pinned to the calmest days.

Model & assumptions

Equations

All quantities in SI (W, m², kg, h) unless noted.

  • Solar declination:  δ(d) = 23.44° · sin(2π·(d−81)/365). Day-of-year d ∈ [1, 365]. Equinoxes near d=81 and d=264.
  • Sunrise hour angle:  cos(H₀) = −tan(φ)·tan(δ). Polar day if right-hand side ≤ −1, polar night if ≥ 1. Daylight hours: N = (24/π)·H₀.
  • Daily horizontal solar energy per m² at the array:
    Esun = (24·S/π)·csky·[H₀·sin(φ)·sin(δ) + cos(φ)·cos(δ)·sin(H₀)]  [Wh/m²]
    S = 1361 W/m² (top-of-atmosphere; Kopp & Lean 2011, SORCE/TIM). csky is the user-set clear-sky fraction. Standard horizontal-surface insolation integral – see Duffie & Beckman, Solar Engineering of Thermal Processes.
  • Daily energy from beam:  Ebeam = 24·Ppeak·ηcap·duty·csky  [Wh]
    Ppeak is the user-set received power (electrical at the HAPS bus, ideal conditions). Capture factor ηcap = min(1, Arx / Aspot) caps delivery when the receiver is smaller than the beam footprint. Atmospheric loss and PV conversion are absorbed into Ppeak: a 100 kW transmitter typically delivers ~14–47 kW electrical to the bus depending on receiver type (Landis on laser-power converters; Fraunhofer ISE GaAs monochromatic tables). Arx = 0 ⇒ zero delivered power.
  • Daily energy demand:  Edemand = 24·(Pcruise + Ppayload). Cruise power taken from the platform anchor; payload from the slider.
  • Operability of day d:  Esun(d) + Ebeam ≥ Edemand. Counted across 365 days for the operable-days-per-year KPI.
  • Night energy gap and battery sizing:  Enight = (24−N)·(Pcruise+Ppayload) − Ebeam·(24−N)/24;
    mbattery = max(0, Enight) / DoD / (Wh/kg), with DoD = 0.8 and 250 Wh/kg pack specific energy (mature Li-ion).
  • Wind / gust tolerance scaling:  vgust ∝ √(W/S) (Anderson, Aircraft Performance and Design, 1999; standard FAR Part 23 gust-load analysis). Calibrated so that a light-solar-plane wing-loading of ~3.4 kg/m² gives a ~8 kn calm-air launch limit.

Architecture parameters

  • Ground-up:  Aspot = 2 m² (small footprint at 20–80 km slant),  duty = 1.0 (continuous within line-of-sight and clear sky).
  • Space-down:  Aspot = 20 m² (larger footprint at LEO range),  duty = 0.15 (averaged across a single transmitter's contact windows; a multi-satellite constellation can raise this).

Platform-class anchors

Each class is calibrated against a representative real-world platform from published manufacturer programme materials. Cruise figures are continuous electrical draw to hold altitude (propulsion + avionics), distinct from each platform's published peak generation rating.

  • Light solar plane – calibrated to Airbus Zephyr-S: 75 kg maximum takeoff weight, 22 m² wing, 25 m² solar array, ~1.5 kW peak generation, ~0.3 kW continuous cruise, 5 kg payload.
  • Mid solar plane – calibrated to BAE Systems PHASA-35: 150 kg maximum takeoff weight, 35 m² wing, 50 m² array, ~3 kW peak, ~0.6 kW continuous cruise, 15 kg payload.
  • Heavy solar plane – calibrated to AeroVironment / HAPSMobile Sunglider: 1000 kg maximum takeoff weight, 130 m² wing, 130 m² array, ~7 kW peak, ~1.8 kW continuous cruise, 70 kg payload.
  • Stratospheric airship – calibrated to Sceye Inc.: 800 kg maximum takeoff weight, 240 m² envelope reference, 240 m² array, ~10 kW peak, ~4 kW continuous (includes pressure management), 250 kg payload.

Other physical constants

  • Solar irradiance (TOA): S = 1361 W/m² – Kopp & Lean (2011), Geophys. Res. Lett. 38, L01706.
  • HAPS atmosphere at 20 km: ρ ≈ 0.089 kg/m³, p ≈ 5.5 kPa, T ≈ 217 K – U.S. Standard Atmosphere 1976 (NOAA/NASA/USAF).
  • Array efficiency on the HAPS itself: ηPVsun = 25% (triple-junction GaAs cells under AM0; Fraunhofer ISE published efficiency tables).
  • Diffraction limit cited in §3: D ≈ 2.44·λ·L / dspot – Born & Wolf, Principles of Optics, §8.5.
  • Battery technology: 250 Wh/kg, 80% useful DoD (mature aerospace Li-ion).

Simplifications. Horizontal-array model (no Sun-tracking, no cosine loss against wing tilt). Steady-state daily energy balance (no battery state-of-charge dynamics). Atmospheric absorption modelled as a single transmission factor per architecture. Ground-station LOS, pointing budget, and per-pass orbital geometry are absorbed into the duty-cycle factor. Cruise power held constant across season and altitude.
