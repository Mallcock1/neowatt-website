Section 1 · The constraint

Thruster power scales steeply with altitude.

Drag rises sharply as you descend into VLEO, and thruster power follows – from ~300 W at 250 km to ~4 kW at 150 km. An order of magnitude in 100 km.

For a fixed power budget, that curve is a hard altitude floor.

Thruster power vs altitude

Section 2 · The power-limited floor

Your budget sets the lowest altitude you can hold.

A 1.6 kW bus with 0.8 kW for payload leaves 0.8 kW for thrusters. Where that line meets the demand curve – around 205 km – is your practical limit.

Going lower means more solar or more battery. Both add drag and mass – fighting the goal.

Today's operating envelope

Section 3 · Going lower

Beamed power lowers the altitude floor.

NEOWATT beams laser power from a transmitter satellite in sunlight to the receiver. Even 0.3 kW delivered to the bus shifts the floor.

At 0.5 kW, expect 15–20 km lower without resizing. At 1 kW, sub-180 km becomes feasible.

With supplemental power

Section 4 · Through eclipse

Longer payload duty cycle in the eclipse.

In eclipse, payload draw comes from batteries. High-power instruments – IR, radar, active comms – drain capacity fast. Beamed power from a transmitter still in sunlight cuts the net battery drain.

VLEO eclipses last 30–35 min. A partial supplement is the difference between a curtailed and a complete pass.

Section 5 · Breaking the death spiral

A larger bus, without the engineering cascade.

Bigger bus → more drag → more power required → bigger solar array → more drag → more power required → …

Beamed power lands on the bus without adding drag (or via a small receiver – a fraction of an array's footprint). It's the only input that breaks the loop.

The result: more sustainable payload at every altitude, and feasibility where the loop otherwise rules you out.

Model & assumptions

Equations

All quantities in SI (W, m², m/s, kg/m³, s) unless noted.

  • Electrical thruster power to hold altitude against drag:
    Pthr(h, A) = K · ρ(h) · v² · A  [W]
    Derived from Fdrag = ½·Cd·ρ·v²·A combined with an ion-thruster power model Pelec = Fdrag·vexh/(2ηthr), giving K = Cd·vexh/(4ηthr). Calibrated to K = 32 873 m/s so Pthr(200 km, 2 m²) = 1 kW — implies vexh/(4ηthr) ≈ 14.9 km/s (consistent with Hall-effect/ion thrusters with vexh ~20–40 km/s and ηthr ~0.5–0.6). Cd = 2.2 (free-molecular flat plate, Cook 1965; Moe & Moe 2005) is absorbed into K.
  • Atmospheric density (piecewise log-linear between tabulated anchors):
    ρ(h) = exp( lnρi + (lnρi+1 − lnρi)·(h − hi)/(hi+1 − hi) )  [kg/m³]
    Anchors (km, kg/m³): (130, 5×10⁻⁹), (150, 1×10⁻⁹), (200, 2.5×10⁻¹⁰), (250, 7×10⁻¹¹), (300, 2.3×10⁻¹¹), (350, 9.5×10⁻¹²), (450, 2.2×10⁻¹²). The 150–250 km values are Crisp-consistent; 130/300/350/450 km are NRLMSISE-00 nominal (F10.7 ≈ 140). Implied scale height grows from ~36 km at 150 km altitude to ~68 km at 450 km — consistent with the real thermosphere.
  • Orbital period (circular two-body):
    T = 2π √((R⊕+h)³ / μ⊕)  [s]
    R⊕ = 6371 km,  μ⊕ = 3.986×10⁵ km³/s². Enter h in km and T comes out in seconds.
  • Eclipse duration:  tecl = T · (fecl/100)  (same units as T). The user sets the orbit fraction in shadow as a percentage.
  • Payload runtime in eclipse (battery-limited, capped at eclipse duration):
    tbase = min( tecl,  Ebat / Ppayload )
    taug  = min( tecl,  Ebat / max(0, Ppayload − Pdelivered) )
    Ebat in Wh and P in W gives time in hours — multiply by 60 for minutes. Simplifications: (i) thrusters are assumed off in eclipse (many VLEO concepts thrust only in sunlight to conserve battery); (ii) battery starts eclipse fully charged; (iii) beaming is available in shadow (transmitter remains in sunlight, e.g. dawn–dusk SSO).
  • Sustainable payload at altitude h:
    Ppayload, max = Aarray·S·ηPV + Pdelivered − Pthr(h, Aarray+Abus+Arcv)  [W]
    Steady state: solar generation plus beam minus drag-power draw. Negative ⇒ the platform cannot close its power budget at this altitude. Simplification: solar generation assumes the array is normal to the Sun (no cosine loss); a body-mounted array would effectively give ~60% of this value on average.
  • Lowest operable altitude (floor): the altitude at which Pthr(h, A) = Pavail, solved by bisection. Available thrust power is Pbudget − Ppayload for the baseline, plus Pdelivered when beaming is on. Baseline drag area is Aarray+Abus; the beaming case adds Arcv when the specialised receiver is selected.
  • Required incident power at the receiver (illustrative — atmospheric and geometric losses neglected):
    Prx = Pdelivered / ηreceiver.

Assumption values

  • Orbital velocity: v = 7.8 km/s, held constant. v varies ~2.4% (7.83 → 7.64 km/s) over 130–450 km, dwarfed by the >100× density variation that dominates drag.
  • Solar flux: S = 1361 W/m² — total solar irradiance, Kopp & Lean (2011) SORCE/TIM. PV efficiency: ηPV = 30% (triple-junction GaAs, e.g. Spectrolab XTJ Prime).
  • Generic-panel receiver: ηreceiver = 25%, Arcv = 0 (beam onto the existing array). Representative of Si/III–V single-wavelength response.
  • Specialised receiver: ηreceiver = 60%, Arcv = 0.3 m² × (1 + δdrag/100) (the slider adds a Cd penalty). Consistent with reported wavelength-tuned PV demonstrations (Steinsiek et al.; LaserMotive).
  • Calibration benchmark: Pthr calibrated on the VLEO drag–power data summarised in Crisp et al. (2020), The benefits of very low earth orbit for earth observation missions. Exact at 200 km on the reference 2 m² platform; ~10.7% low at 150 km, ~6.7% low at 250 km against the published points.
