Capability 1 · Lower Altitude

Thruster power scales steeply with altitude.

Drag rises sharply as you descend into VLEO, and thruster power follows - from ~300 W at 250 km to ~4 kW at 150 km. An order of magnitude in 100 km.

For a fixed power budget, that curve is a hard altitude floor.

Capability 1 · Lower Altitude

Your power budget sets the floor. Beaming lowers it.

A 1.6 kW bus with 0.8 kW for payload leaves 0.8 kW for thrusters. The altitude where that headroom runs out - around 208 km - is your practical limit. Going lower means more solar or more battery, both adding drag and mass.

NEOWATT beams laser power from a transmitter satellite in sunlight to the receiver. Even 0.3 kW delivered to the bus shifts the floor. At 0.5 kW, expect 15–20 km lower without resizing. At 1 kW, sub-180 km becomes feasible.

Capability 2 · Eclipse Runtime

Longer payload duty cycle in the eclipse.

In eclipse, payload draw comes from batteries. High-power instruments - IR, radar, active comms - drain capacity fast. Beamed power from a transmitter still in sunlight cuts the net battery drain.

VLEO eclipses last ~31 min. Without beaming, batteries may only sustain the payload for ~22 min (71%). With even modest supplemental power, the payload operates through the full eclipse - 100%.

Capability 3 · Larger Bus

A larger bus, without the engineering cascade.

Bigger bus → more drag → more power required → bigger solar array → more drag → more power required → …

Beamed power lands on the bus without adding drag (or via a small receiver - a fraction of an array's footprint). It's the only input that breaks the loop.

The result: more sustainable payload mass at every altitude, and feasibility where the loop otherwise rules you out.

Capability 4 · Geomagnetic Storm Resilience

Design for typical days. Beam through the storms.

Atmospheric drag at VLEO varies with space weather. A severe geomagnetic storm can increase drag - and the power needed to hold altitude - to around 5× above a quiet day, for a few hours a few times a year.

With conventional approaches to VLEO, you overbuild the platform for that worst case and fly an oversized, overpriced satellite the rest of the time. With power beaming, you size for typical conditions and receive the extra power to maintain orbit during a geomagnetic storm - a smaller, lighter, cheaper satellite with the same resilience.

Space weather drives thruster power

Drag in VLEO is driven mostly by space weather, and space weather events follow a heavy-tailed distribution. So the thruster power needed to hold orbit varies wildly: most of the time it sits near the median, but severe geomagnetic storms push it far into the tail. Onboard thrusters cover demand up to their limit; beyond that the beam supplies the rest.

Model & assumptions

Equations

All quantities in SI (W, m², m/s, kg/m³, s) unless noted.

  • Electrical thruster power to hold altitude against drag:
    Pthr(h, A) = K · ρ(h) · v² · A  [W]
    Derived from Fdrag = ½·Cd·ρ·v²·A combined with an ion-thruster power model Pelec = Fdrag·vexh/(2ηthr), giving K = Cd·vexh/(4ηthr). Calibrated to K = 32 873 m/s so Pthr(200 km, 2 m²) = 1 kW - implies vexh/(4ηthr) ≈ 14.9 km/s (consistent with Hall-effect/ion thrusters with vexh ~20–40 km/s and ηthr ~0.5–0.6). Cd = 2.2 (free-molecular flat plate, Cook 1965; Moe & Moe 2005) is absorbed into K.
  • Atmospheric density - cubic Hermite spline in log-density through published anchors, C¹-smooth (no kinks at anchor points):
    Anchors (km, kg/m³): (130, 5×10⁻⁹), (150, 1×10⁻⁹), (200, 2.5×10⁻¹⁰), (250, 7×10⁻¹¹), (300, 2.3×10⁻¹¹), (350, 9.5×10⁻¹²), (450, 2.2×10⁻¹²). The 150–250 km values are Crisp-consistent; 130/300/350/450 km are NRLMSISE-00 nominal (F10.7 ≈ 140). Below 130 km and above 450 km the spline falls back to the outermost segment's log-slope.
  • Orbital period (circular two-body):
    T = 2π √((R⊕+h)³ / μ⊕)  [s]
    R⊕ = 6371 km,  μ⊕ = 3.986×10⁵ km³/s². Enter h in km and T comes out in seconds.
  • Eclipse duration:  tecl = T · (fecl/100)  (same units as T). The user sets the orbit fraction in shadow as a percentage.
  • Payload runtime in eclipse (battery-limited, capped at eclipse duration):
    tbase = min( tecl,  Ebat / Ppayload )
    taug  = min( tecl,  Ebat / max(0, Ppayload − Pdelivered) )
    Ebat in Wh and P in W gives time in hours - multiply by 60 for minutes. Simplifications: (i) thrusters are assumed off in eclipse (many VLEO concepts thrust only in sunlight to conserve battery); (ii) battery starts eclipse fully charged; (iii) beaming is available in shadow (transmitter remains in sunlight, e.g. dawn–dusk SSO).
  • Sustainable payload at altitude h:
    Ppayload, max = Aarray·S·ηPV + Pdelivered − Pthr(h, Aarray+Abus+Arcv)  [W]
    Steady state: solar generation plus beam minus drag-power draw. Negative ⇒ the platform cannot close its power budget at this altitude. Simplification: solar generation assumes the array is normal to the Sun (no cosine loss); a body-mounted array would effectively give ~60% of this value on average.
  • Lowest operable altitude (floor): the altitude at which Pthr(h, A) = Pavail, solved by bisection. Available thrust power is Pbudget − Ppayload for the baseline, plus Pdelivered when beaming is on. Baseline drag area is Aarray+Abus; the beaming case adds Arcv when the specialised receiver is selected.
  • Required incident power at the receiver (illustrative - atmospheric and geometric losses neglected):
    Prx = Pdelivered / ηreceiver.

Assumption values

  • Orbital velocity: v = 7.8 km/s, held constant. v varies ~2.4% (7.83 → 7.64 km/s) over 130–450 km, dwarfed by the ~2000× density variation that dominates drag.
  • Solar flux: S = 1361 W/m² - total solar irradiance, Kopp & Lean (2011) SORCE/TIM. PV efficiency: ηPV = 30% (triple-junction GaAs, e.g. Spectrolab XTJ Prime).
  • Generic-panel receiver: ηreceiver = 25%, Arcv = 0 (beam onto the existing array). Representative of Si/III–V single-wavelength response.
  • Specialised receiver: ηreceiver = 60%, Arcv = 0.3 m² × (1 + δdrag/100) (the slider adds a Cd penalty). Consistent with reported wavelength-tuned PV demonstrations (Steinsiek et al.; LaserMotive).
  • Calibration benchmark: Pthr calibrated on the VLEO drag–power data summarised in Crisp et al. (2020), The benefits of very low earth orbit for earth observation missions. On the reference 2 m² platform the model gives 4 kW at 150 km, 1 kW at 200 km, and 280 W at 250 km - matching Crisp's 4 kW / 1 kW / 300 W reference points to within 7%. Interpolated densities between anchors agree with NRLMSISE-00 nominal values to within ~20%.

Model & assumptions

Variability model

Thruster power at altitude h is modelled as P(h) · M, where M is a log-normal multiplier with μ=0 and a fixed log-space spread σ = 0.50 (see below). The median of M is 1 (i.e. the calibrated nominal power); the right tail captures combined solar-cycle and geomagnetic-storm variability.

Why log-normal, and why σ = 0.50?

Thermospheric density at a fixed altitude is driven by solar (F10.7) and geomagnetic activity and is approximately log-normal over multi-year horizons. The size of the swing grows strongly with altitude: Emmert (2015) reports roughly a factor of 10 over a solar cycle near 400 km, but at the 200–250 km altitudes relevant here it is markedly smaller, of order a factor of 4–5. Mapping that VLEO-scale swing to the 5th–95th percentiles of the long-term distribution gives σ ≈ 0.50, with geomagnetic storms populating the rarer right tail. Reference: Emmert (2015), Thermospheric mass density: A review, Adv. Space Res. 56, 773.

Availability model

For an available thruster power C, the platform can hold its orbit whenever demand is at or below C. The share of the time this holds is read directly from the demand distribution: availability = Φ( ln(C / P(h)) / σ ), where Φ is the standard-normal CDF. Time in deficit over the mission = (1 − availability) × mission duration. Beamed power raises C from the onboard value to onboard + beam during storms; a residual deficit remains only when demand exceeds even that combined total.

Why no storm rate?

Availability is a property of the distribution alone, so it needs no assumption about how often storms strike or how long they last. The trade-off: a time-in-deficit figure does not by itself say whether a single deficit lasts long enough to be mission-ending - that depends on how a given deficit's duration compares with the platform's altitude margin. It is the clean, assumption-free measure of how often the platform is short of power.
